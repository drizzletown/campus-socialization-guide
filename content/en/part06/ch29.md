# Chapter 29. Competitions and Interdisciplinary Projects

Team members argue about titles, while technical interfaces, eligibility and submission rights remain unclear.

## A competition result is only one part of what was learned

Competitions combine a deadline, competing approaches and shared technical responsibilities. A team may learn a great deal and still lose; a winning solution may conceal weak documentation or unfair division of work. Cross-disciplinary teams need a common description of the problem, interfaces, assumptions and acceptance criteria before dividing tasks. Organizers and members should also clarify tool-use rules, costs, intellectual property and health-related limits. The durable achievement is the capacity to form a defensible solution together under constraints, not simply a line on a certificate.

Different disciplines contribute through different evidence: code, experiments, analysis, user studies, writing or risk assessment. A contest rank cannot by itself measure each person's capability.

Competition teams often combine students with different skills, ambitions and available time. Everyone may want to perform well while disagreeing about training frequency, travel costs, team selection and ownership of shared work. Establish the competition's eligibility rules, responsibilities, code-management practices and procedure for changing roles early. Roles may evolve as skills develop, but selection should rely on explicit criteria and reproducible evidence rather than personal loyalty.

Disputes intensify when places are scarce or one member controls a crucial technical component. Fair selection and a clear review process matter more than forcing everyone to agree privately with the captain. A disappointing result may reflect algorithmic gaps, time pressure, communication errors, inadequate preparation or chance. An award is one outcome, not a complete explanation of the learning process. A team that documents solutions, shares knowledge and reviews failures constructively may create lasting opportunities even without a medal. Departing members should make legitimate handovers while protecting restricted materials and respecting competition and institutional rules.

A genuine skill mismatch needs training or reassignment. Repeated failure to deliver despite clear agreements requires a fair accountability process.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: read competition rules. Then define outputs and owners. Next set shared review points. Finally document contribution and intellectual-property arrangements.

Do not submit another person's work, violate competition rules or sacrifice safety and study obligations merely for a prize.
