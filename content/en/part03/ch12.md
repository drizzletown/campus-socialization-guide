# Chapter 12. Time, Energy and Commitments: Busy Is Not Always Better

You join several projects, keep agreeing to help friends, and find that sleep and coursework are suffering.

## Reliability starts before saying yes

A student may accept a club post, a competition, a part-time job and demanding courses within the same week. The resulting conflict is not always a failure of calendar technique. It can be a problem of overlapping roles and unrealistic expectations. Before committing, identify actual workload, deadlines, dependencies and possible exit arrangements. When circumstances change, early notice and an honest handover are often more responsible than heroic last-minute efforts. Rest is part of a sustainable commitment, not a reward that can only be earned after exhaustion.

Every new role creates communication and delivery costs. Growth depends on the value and sustainability of participation, not the number of hours booked. An existing promise deserves care; a new invitation may be evaluated before acceptance.

A crowded schedule can look like evidence of growth. A student may take difficult courses, join research, lead a club, work part-time and prepare applications, all while having no uninterrupted time to practice core skills. The visible number of activities says little about what is being learned. Begin with real capacity: classes, travel, sleep, meals, essential responsibilities and a margin for unexpected problems. Only then evaluate a new commitment against its likely benefits and opportunity costs.

Declining an additional role need not be a defense of your personality. Explain what you have already promised, what limited help you could realistically provide, and when you can reconsider. For existing commitments, notify others early enough to reallocate work; leaving without handing over responsibilities creates costs for them. Also ask whether a task genuinely requires your personal involvement. Some work can be delegated, rescheduled, scaled down or cancelled. If an organization depends on students' repeated unpaid overwork, the organizational arrangement needs revision, not merely better personal productivity. Time management is about making chosen responsibilities sustainable, not maximizing the number of entries on a résumé.

A one-off busy week may need scheduling. Ongoing demands without authority, training or rest may call for renegotiation or withdrawal.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: list non-negotiable needs and confirmed duties. Then estimate the true cost of optional work. Next ask for scope before committing. Finally give early notice if a deadline becomes unrealistic.

Do not make sleep, health or safety the default source of spare time. Seek support if stress seriously affects everyday functioning.
