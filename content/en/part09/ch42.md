# Chapter 42. Harassment, Coercion and Control: Put Safety First

Someone with influence over your grades, housing or social life pressures you to comply and threatens consequences if you refuse.

## Coercion changes the conditions for agreement

Ordinary disagreement assumes that both people can express preferences without fear of retaliation. Stalking, threats to disclose intimate information or pressure based on academic authority can remove that freedom. A seemingly voluntary agreement may therefore be unsafe to interpret at face value. Prioritize immediate safety, appropriate confidential support and the relevant protection procedure. Do not insist on a face-to-face reconciliation when a person is afraid. Friends should offer informed accompaniment rather than force public disclosure or undertake vigilante retaliation.

Harassment and coercion are not ordinary failures of social skill. Assess immediate danger, dependence on the person, available safe alternatives and the role of institutional power.

Harassment and coercive control are sometimes minimized as interpersonal intensity. Examine patterns: repeated conduct, disregard of a clear refusal, threats, monitoring and exploitation of dependence. Demands for phone access, attempts to isolate a person from friends, waiting outside their residence or threatening to publish intimate material are not ordinary communication misunderstandings. An affected student does not have to arrange repeated private meetings to persuade the other person to stop. Reducing contact, preserving relevant evidence and contacting trusted or protected support can be appropriate.

Do not apply “talk it through face to face” automatically where stalking, violence, privacy threats or institutional retaliation are possible. A private confrontation may create further danger. Consider a safe location, accompaniment, formal reporting or local emergency assistance as circumstances require. Friends should respect the affected person's wishes and avoid publicizing the case without consent. Universities need processes that address conflicts of interest when a person with educational authority is implicated. Exercising autonomy includes using protective institutions rather than having to confront a threatening person alone.

A respectful disagreement can be handled through communication. Stalking, threats, blackmail or forced contact call for protective services and documented reporting.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: move to safety when necessary. Then avoid unnecessary contact with the person. Next preserve essential evidence without taking new risks. Finally seek authorized campus or external support.

You need not confront a dangerous person or gather perfect evidence before requesting help. In immediate danger, contact local emergency services.
