# Chapter 39. Building Professional Connections Without Trading Favors

You want to ask an alumnus about their work but fear that networking means requesting personal favors.

## Professional relationships are not collections of favors

A good professional question is specific: what does this role involve, which technical choice matters, or what could be improved in a demonstrated project? Respect the other person's time and their authority to decline. Informal contact can clarify a field but should not replace public procedures or justify confidential disclosures. With continued reliable work, a student may become a contributor rather than only a requester. Professional networks grow through shared practice, clear boundaries and verified competence, not through personal flattery.

Professional ties can offer information, feedback and different perspectives, but they do not create an obligation to provide referrals. Public career services remain valid, often more transparent routes.

Professional networking is often mistaken for asking strangers for favors. It can instead begin with a legitimate shared question. If an engineer publishes a technical article, a concise message describing a reproducible result or a specific difficulty can be more appropriate than a long biography followed by a demand for a referral. Check the person's public role, explain why the question is relevant and accept that they may be unable to respond. Professional networks also include public communities, careers services and formal application channels—not just powerful individuals.

A referral does not replace checking a job's requirements or legitimacy. A person who introduces an opportunity cannot guarantee an outcome they do not control. Over time, credible professional relationships grow from useful exchanges, clear commitments and respect for limited attention. Share work that you are entitled to make public; thank people for feedback without exposing private contact details or conversations. Connections can help your knowledge reach real problems, but they do not create ownership over another person's time, influence or decision-making authority.

A narrow, well-researched question may invite a useful exchange. Pressuring someone for an internal referral or a guaranteed job crosses a boundary.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: read publicly available information. Then request a brief discussion on a specific question. Next respect a refusal. Finally act on useful feedback and acknowledge the person's time.

Never claim that a casual introduction is official recruitment approval. Reciprocity should not be confused with hidden payment or personal indebtedness.
