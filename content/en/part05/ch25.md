# Chapter 25. Leaving a Group or Changing Your Role

A project that began as occasional volunteering now demands daily attendance and criticizes anyone who withdraws.

## Leaving a role is part of learning responsibility

Circumstances change after a student accepts a position or project. Responsible departure means identifying current duties, outstanding commitments and items that need transfer. It does not mean remaining indefinitely because others have failed to plan succession. A well-run group should be able to replace members, document processes and agree on reasonable handovers. Where participation has become dangerous or coercive, safety may require immediate withdrawal and formal help. Entry, cooperation and departure all belong to the same social learning process.

Participation is sustainable only when duties, learning value, resources and freedom to change are reasonably aligned. Leaving can be a responsible response when circumstances change.

Leaving a team can feel like choosing between personal wellbeing and responsibility to others. The key is to separate the reason for leaving from the commitments still outstanding. If an activity no longer fits your goals, give reasonable notice, complete an agreed handover and stop accepting new work. If the environment includes harassment, threats or coercive demands, safety comes first; you may need formal protection rather than another private confrontation. A member should be evaluated against actual commitments, not punished simply for deciding to leave.

A useful handover identifies the location and authorized owner of project files, outstanding tasks, relevant deadlines and people who can approve changes. Institutional data and access credentials must be transferred or removed according to policy. But a handover has an endpoint: being the only person who understands a system does not create an unlimited unpaid obligation after graduation. When no replacement exists, the organization should reduce scope, extend timelines or provide training. Ending an arrangement competently is part of the capacity to take on future collaborations with clearer expectations and healthier boundaries.

If the burden is temporary and meaningful, negotiate scope. If promised support never appears or demands become coercive, pursue an orderly exit or seek institutional help.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: review the original commitment. Then identify remaining deliverables. Next propose a realistic handover. Finally document agreements and protect confidential information.

Do not abandon urgent safety obligations without arranging proper transfer. Others cannot demand unlimited service as proof of loyalty.
