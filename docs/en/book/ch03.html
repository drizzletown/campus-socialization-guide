Chapter 03. Five Dimensions: Capability, Networks, Roles, Contribution and Autonomy

A student completes difficult project work but receives no credit and cannot refuse additional tasks.

Why capability, access and authority must be separated

Two applicants can experience the same rejection for different reasons. One may have strong technical work but no access to the application notice; another may know team members but lack the prerequisite skills. A third may join yet receive no clear responsibility. These are not interchangeable deficits. Contribution must also be kept distinct from personal worth: students deserve respect and legitimate support before producing visible results. A role may increase without increasing freedom to refuse, and a larger network may create more obligations rather than more genuine options. The five dimensions organize evidence about one situation; they are not numerical scores.

The SSCM model distinguishes C, N, R, V and A. C is demonstrated capability; N is relevant, reachable support; R is responsibility and decision authority; V is the actual effect of participation; A is the ability to choose and renegotiate with reasons.

Imagine a failed group project. You have the technical ability to analyze data (C) and have joined the team chat (N), yet nobody assigns you a defined task (R), so no deliverable can be attributed to your participation (V). The remedy is not automatically “try harder.” You may need a clear allocation process, permission to claim work and an agreed acceptance criterion. Now imagine that you become the principal contributor but cannot refuse additional demands for fear of losing friendships. Contribution increases while your freedom to revise commitments (A) may decrease. The five observations reveal different dimensions of the same experience.

Avoid turning these observations into personal grades. “I failed to reproduce the database benchmark independently” is more useful than “I have low ability”; “I cannot find an open application route” is more actionable than “I have no network.” Each observation should be grounded in a particular goal and its conditions. Contribution means an effect within an activity, not a person's worth. Students are entitled to rest, care and legitimate institutional support without first earning them by being useful. The framework supports situated reasoning; it is neither a maturity ranking nor an exhaustive theory of every emotional experience.

Good skills do not guarantee recognition. A visible contribution does not prove that the person has reasonable freedom to adjust commitments.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: record the objective and current constraints. Then note evidence for each of the five dimensions. Next locate the specific mismatch. Finally choose a correction that addresses that mismatch.

These dimensions are not numerical scores, personality types, or a hierarchy. The value of a person is not measured by productivity.
