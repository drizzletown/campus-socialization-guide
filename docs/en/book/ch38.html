Chapter 38. Job Hunting: Match Evidence to Real Work

You send applications without responses and begin to believe you have no worthwhile skills.

A job application must connect evidence to real work

Formatting a résumé matters, but employers need evidence of someone who can address a specific set of tasks. Describe a project by its problem, method, validation and your actual responsibility. Investigate the role, working conditions, contract and recruitment authority before sharing sensitive documents. Requests for deposits or unrelated financial information warrant caution. Rejection can reflect an unavailable role, changing requirements or poor fit, rather than a judgment on personal worth. A student should evaluate whether a job deserves acceptance as carefully as an employer evaluates whether a candidate fits.

Recruitment outcomes depend on opportunity supply, timing, qualification filters and how evidence matches role requirements. A rejection does not identify its cause by itself.

Unsuccessful job applications can lead to two equally unhelpful explanations: “I am not good enough” or “the entire market is unfair.” Break the process into finding reliable vacancies, matching qualifications, presenting evidence of skill, interviewing and checking employment terms. Silence after an application may reflect competition or recruitment timing; a demand for an upfront training deposit is a different category of issue requiring risk assessment, not confidence coaching.

Choose several realistic role families and examine their published requirements. Replace vague résumé adjectives with demonstrable work: the task, your contribution, the method and a result that can legitimately be described. Where skills are missing, build a targeted learning plan; where criteria are ambiguous, seek clarification through an official contact. Record actual interview questions to learn from them without turning every rejection into a global self-judgment. Verify employer identity, compensation, working arrangements and data requests. Requests for passwords, one-time bank codes or transfers to personal accounts are serious warning signs. A good first job decision is not only about securing an offer; it is about understanding what you are agreeing to.

A resume with unclear project evidence needs revision. A fee-demanding recruiter or request for bank codes needs independent fraud checks rather than better self-promotion.

Two unsuccessful job searches with different causes

Student A sends twenty applications and receives no interviews. Comparing vacancies with the résumé reveals that the projects are described only as “familiar with data analysis,” without methods, scale or verifiable outcomes. The next round can present more relevant evidence for each role and include one independently explainable project. If responses remain scarce, investigate changing demand and application channels. Silence is information about a process, not a personality diagnosis.

Student B quickly receives an interview invitation, followed by a promise of likely employment on condition that a training fee be paid to a personal account and identity documents be uploaded. Interview practice is not the immediate priority. The student should stop payment and disclosure, verify the employer using authoritative channels and seek appropriate careers or anti-fraud support. Both students report a difficult job search, yet the appropriate response is completely different. Career judgment includes knowing when to improve skills, when to change strategy and when to leave a suspicious process.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: read the job's actual duties. Then map them to verifiable projects. Next describe your role and outcomes honestly. Finally check employer legitimacy and contract conditions.

Do not submit confidential employer materials or invented experience. Never pay an unverified recruiter merely for the promise of a job.
