Chapter 09. Make Group Assignments Reliable and Fair

Only two members finish a group project while others neither reply nor deliver, and the deadline is close.

The hidden work in a team is integration

Dividing a project into four jobs does not guarantee a coherent result. An interface depends on an API; testing depends on a working build; documentation depends on decisions that the team actually made. Reliable teamwork identifies dependencies, acceptance criteria and change-notification rules early. Unequal contributions need interpretation: the difficulty may concern access, preparation, a genuine hardship or repeated refusal to fulfil an agreement. The response should vary accordingly. If unresolved, a factual record and the course's formal procedures are more appropriate than humiliation or last-minute silent rescue.

Group failure may arise from unclear tasks, mismatched skills, inaccessible tools, undefined decision rights or unwillingness to contribute. Evidence about tasks matters more than calling someone lazy.

A group assignment is not simply a set of equal-sized chores. In a four-person software project, one student designs an API, another builds the interface, a third tests the system and a fourth documents decisions. If the API changes without notice, other tasks may fail even though everyone has been busy. A dependable plan identifies dependencies, expected deliverables, who checks them and how changes are communicated. Agree on a minimum viable result early enough to uncover integration problems before the final submission.

Unequal participation has different causes. Someone may lack repository access, be unable to perform an assigned task, face an unexpected hardship or repeatedly ignore an agreement. Start by clarifying the task and offering an appropriate path to ask for support. Keep a factual change history rather than turning the team chat into a public trial. If reasonable attempts fail, refer to the course's rules for documenting contribution. Students who take on additional work should be able to renegotiate scope, deadlines and attribution. Cooperation does not require identical personalities or perfectly equal outputs; it requires understandable commitments, fair procedures for change and a result that the team can explain and verify.

If a member lacks access or training, fix that barrier. If duties are documented and repeatedly ignored, use the course's formal contribution and escalation rules.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: agree on deliverables, owners and dates. Then use a shared progress log. Next check blockers without public accusations. Finally adjust assignments or ask the instructor for guidance.

Do not silently complete everything while erasing the actual contributions of others. Do not circulate private accusations or fabricate work records.
