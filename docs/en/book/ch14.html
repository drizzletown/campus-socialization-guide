Chapter 14. Mental and Physical Well-Being: Asking for Help Early

You have been sleeping badly, missing classes and withdrawing, yet worry you are not unwell enough to seek assistance.

Health is a condition of participation, not a performance score

Academic difficulty can arise from an interaction among knowledge gaps, workload, sleep, housing and health. A single instruction to work harder may miss the relevant condition. Support includes ordinary adjustments, trusted company, appropriate academic accommodations and qualified clinical services; none fully replaces the others. A friend can listen without becoming a clinician. Students should know how to access professional help and what confidentiality rules apply. Immediate danger requires urgent services, not a self-improvement exercise. Institutions share responsibility for making support safe and reachable.

Health affects action capacity but does not define a person's social worth. Friends can offer care; clinicians and qualified services provide assessment and treatment. The level of support should match the concern and local resources.

Mental and physical health should not be reduced to a judgment about toughness. A brief period of exam anxiety, several weeks of disrupted sleep and an inability to manage daily activities are different situations. Describe what has changed, how long it has lasted and what functioning is affected instead of diagnosing yourself from a checklist. Friends can listen and accompany someone, but they cannot replace qualified assessment when symptoms are serious. A difficult day is also not, by itself, proof of a disorder.

When seeking academic accommodation, identify a practical need and verify the formal route: counselling appointments, disability support, medical leave or an examination arrangement, as applicable. Instructors may offer help, but changes to official requirements must follow institutional policy. If there is an immediate threat of self-harm or another acute emergency, contact local emergency services and trusted people nearby rather than completing a workbook exercise. Supporting a friend can mean staying with them while professional help is arranged; it does not require you to become their sole crisis responder. Rest, treatment and recovery can be important parts of continued development.

For everyday adjustment, start with practical support and available campus services. For acute symptoms or immediate risk, seek qualified or emergency help without waiting for a workbook exercise.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: describe observable changes in sleep, appetite, attendance or functioning. Then find a legitimate support service. Next ask about confidentiality and referral arrangements. Finally make an achievable next contact.

Do not diagnose yourself or others from internet advice. You need not share medical details publicly to deserve respect or reasonable accommodation.
