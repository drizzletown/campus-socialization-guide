Chapter 22. Student Leadership: Match Responsibility with Authority

You become a club officer and suddenly handle money, approvals, personnel issues and tasks no one formally assigned to you.

A title does not automatically supply authority

Student leaders are often assigned every unresolved problem without the power or resources to address it. Clarify what a coordinator can decide, what requires collective approval and what must go through a university office. A budget, contract or safety decision cannot be authorized merely by enthusiasm or a club title. Reliable leadership distributes understanding through records, handovers and realistic responsibilities. When the group can function without one exhausted person holding everything together, its organizational capacity has actually improved.

Leadership is a defined organizational role, not a personal honor that makes you responsible for everything. Decisions, approvals, resources and oversight need clear owners.

Student leadership often begins with “could you coordinate this?” and ends with one person being held responsible for money, venue bookings, registration data, safety and outcomes. These tasks do not carry the same authority. A dependable structure separates execution, authorization, consultation and oversight. The chair may draft a budget, for example, without having the authority to sign an external contract on the university's behalf. Recording which decisions require staff approval or member consent protects everyone involved.

Judge organizational decisions by their process as well as the result. Were relevant records available? Could volunteers decline extra commitments? Were people with a conflict of interest excluded from award decisions? Was expenditure documented? An announcement in a chat does not guarantee that affected members understood the implications. If an unauthorized payment is discovered, stop further commitments and follow the appropriate correction procedure instead of quietly asking a student leader to pay personally to preserve appearances. Effective governance creates continuity across cohorts; it does not depend on a constantly exhausted person who knows all the unwritten rules.

Routine coordination may be delegated. Spending, discipline and access to protected information may require formal authorization and separation of duties.

When an event fails, whose responsibility is it?

A student society advertises a lecture for one hundred participants, but the venue has not been formally approved. The speaker books travel, students register and the person in charge is told to “find a solution,” perhaps by renting a commercial hall personally. The critical question is no longer whether the leader is sufficiently decisive. It is which commitments have actual authorization and what conditions remain unmet. A student representative must not make institutional promises that exceed their mandate.

The leader should verify the approval status, notify those affected promptly, assess postponement, remote delivery or cancellation, and keep relevant expense and communication records. A useful review asks why registration opened before the venue was secured, who had authority to declare the event confirmed, and how the process should change next time. If institutional decisions were opaque, that also needs correction. It is reasonable to expect a leader to communicate about promises they made; it is not reasonable automatically to transfer every unauthorized financial risk to one student.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: create a responsibility and approval table. Then record decisions and deadlines. Next publish clear financial processes. Finally provide members a way to challenge or decline extra work.

Do not spend money or disclose private records without authority. Resolve conflicts of interest openly and according to applicable rules.
