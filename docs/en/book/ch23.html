Chapter 23. Volunteering and Civic Participation: Measure Real Benefit

A volunteering program promotes hours and photos, but local participants describe different needs from those advertised.

Service must be evaluated from the recipient's side

Volunteering can become a count of hours and photographs, yet the people receiving support need meaningful benefit, consent and appropriate safeguards. Before designing an activity, learn what problem it is intended to address and whether participants want the proposed intervention. Sensitive populations and professional settings require training and oversight. Later, ask what changed and what structural obstacles remain. Good intentions do not substitute for evidence of usefulness, and public services should not depend indefinitely on unsupervised student labor.

Service should respond to recipients' actual priorities, dignity and consent. Hours and certificates document participation, not social impact. Professional needs require professional qualifications.

Volunteer work is often measured by hours and event counts, but hours do not show whether anyone's actual need was met. Imagine students helping older adults use digital public services. A lively event with many photographs can still fail if participants leave unable to complete the intended task. Begin by learning what the recipients need, what they already know and what information they want to keep private. Invite feedback after the activity instead of assuming that attendance proves success. People receiving support have the right to decline photography and should not become material for a student's résumé without consent.

Service also requires competence and authorization. Untrained volunteers should not make medical decisions or undertake high-risk tasks without supervision. Organizers should clarify safety procedures, confidentiality, equipment responsibilities and an emergency contact. Longer-term service needs sustainable schedules and clear handovers. One carefully designed activity with a useful outcome may matter more than repeated attendance without meaningful feedback. Public participation develops judgment when students learn to listen to those affected, recognize their own limits and revise their work on evidence rather than on publicity alone.

A well-organized project offers training, risk planning and feedback. If the activity seems performative or unsafe, ask the organizer to revise its plan.

For this problem, begin with a concrete step: confirm the purpose and beneficiaries. Then ask about permissions and safeguarding. Next carry out an agreed useful task. Finally assess outcomes with those affected.

Do not photograph children, patients or vulnerable people without proper permission. Do not offer clinical, legal or psychological services beyond your competence.
